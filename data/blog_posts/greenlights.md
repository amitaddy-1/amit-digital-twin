Two headlines have been sitting on my desk today, and I have not been able to shake either of them.

The first is a piece of news from a premier Indian engineering institute. Another young student, brilliant enough to have earned a place among the country's best, has taken their own life. There have been too many of these stories this year, and each one feels like a small piece of the future going quiet.

The second is smaller. A quiet Google Trends chart. I typed the word "happiness" into the search bar, set the geography to India, and asked it to plot the last five years. The line is painful to look at. In May 2021, as the country crawled out of the second Covid wave, "happiness" hit an index of 100. As of yesterday, it sits at 36. A steady, unbroken decline, for over five years.

I know these are two very different data points. One is a life ended. The other is a search index. But they are both, in their own way, telling me the same thing. That there is a great deal of red in the world right now. Red lights everywhere. Loss, obstacles, exhaustion, the low steady hum of being told daily that things are going wrong.

Which is why, this week, I re-visited Matthew McConaughey’s Greenlights.

I love Matthew McConaughey. I say this without the slightest embarrassment. Not just the actor. Not just the endless string of quotable interviews. The man himself, the way he thinks. There is a directness to him that most public figures long ago traded for polish. In 2020 he wrote a memoir called Greenlights, and it is the book I keep going back to when the world starts feeling a little too heavy.

The central idea is simple, and beautifully cinematic. Life, McConaughey says, is a sequence of greenlights, yellowlights, and redlights. The greenlights are the moments when things move: an opportunity opens, an idea catches, a door swings wide, a person walks in whom you were meant to meet. The yellowlights are the pauses, the cautions, the moments to think. And the redlights are the walls. The obstacles. The setbacks. The grief.

Here is the part I find quietly powerful. McConaughey argues that with time and perspective, most redlights eventually turn green. The lost job leads to the better job. The failed relationship teaches the shape of the right one. The wall you slam into becomes the wall you eventually climb, or walk around, or lean against for shade. Redlights are not the opposite of greenlights. They are just greenlights in slow motion.

That is a small and lovely idea to hold on to, in the week both of my headlines arrived.

If I had to pull three lessons from Greenlights, they would be these.

One. Most redlights are delayed greenlights. Not all. Some walls are just walls. But most of the obstacles that feel like they will define you turn out to be the beginning of something you could not have named in advance. This is not toxic positivity. It is patience, worn as a habit.

Two. Notice the greenlights already in the room. McConaughey's second point is that we walk past most of ours because we are staring at the redlights. There is almost always more going right than we notice. The people who love us. The work coming through. The small good coffee this morning. If the Google Trends line is falling, it is not because the world is emptier of greenlights. It is because we have got worse at looking.

Three. Just keep livin'. McConaughey's mantra, and my favourite. Not "keep striving." Not "keep grinding." Just keep livin'. Show up tomorrow. Do the next honest thing. The trick, quietly, is refusing to switch yourself off in the moments the world seems to be asking you to.

The young student at the institute did not get to reach a delayed greenlight. That thought will sit with me for a while. And it is a reminder, if any of us needed one, to keep an eye on the people around us, and on ourselves, and on the slow, patient wisdom of light that eventually turns.

McConaughey has, over the years, given us two great catchphrases. One is alright, alright, alright, which is what he mumbles in every second one of his films. The other is just keep livin', which is what he tries to say in every book. I will take the second one for tonight.

Just keep livin'.

title: The Owl and the Arrival Fallacy

This morning, over my first cup of coffee, my phone buzzed politely to inform me that I had just completed my 1500th consecutive day on Duolingo. Fifteen hundred days. In a row. That is more than four years of getting up, tapping through a few exercises, and keeping a small green owl happy.

I did a small, involuntary fist pump. Somewhere in the room, my mother-in-law raised an eyebrow.

The odd thing is this. I do not, at this stage, actually speak French. I can order a coffee. I can insist that the pen belongs to my aunt. I can politely accuse a dog of being small. But if you dropped me in Paris and asked me to argue with a taxi driver, I would be reduced to sign language and hopeful smiling within thirty seconds. What I have, after 1500 days, is not fluency. What I have is a streak.

And here is the thought that arrived, uninvited, alongside my coffee. What have I actually been chasing all these mornings? The French, or the tick in the box?

This is where I want to bring in an idea from the Harvard positive-psychology professor Tal Ben-Shahar, who gave it a lovely name. He called it the arrival fallacy. The arrival fallacy is our persistent, warm, deeply human belief that reaching a goal will produce lasting happiness. The promotion, the marathon, the weight loss, the corner office, the 1500-day streak: each of these arrives wrapped in the promise that once we get there, we will feel different for a good long while.

In practice, we do not. The moment of arrival gives us a small, bright dopamine spike, which fades before we have finished celebrating. Then we look around, notice the next hill, and start climbing. This is why the CEO who spent thirty years chasing the corner office often looks slightly bewildered when he finally gets there. Not disappointed exactly. Just quietly asking, is this it?

If that were the whole story, it would be a mild kind of foolishness. But there is a second character in this drama, and his contribution turns the arrival fallacy into something much more expensive.

Enter Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, and their 1979 gift to us called prospect theory. Their central finding is that losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. Losing a hundred rupees you had feels considerably worse than earning a hundred you did not have earlier. This is called loss aversion, and it is one of the best-documented facts about the human mind.

Now put the two together. The arrival fallacy tells us that the next goal will finally deliver the happiness we have been waiting for, so we chase it. Prospect theory ensures that if we fail to reach it, or lose it after we have it, the pain outweighs whatever pleasure the pursuit ever contained. The net result is a small, elegant misery machine. We chase goals that will not satisfy us if we hit them, and we suffer disproportionately if we miss them.

Duolingo, incidentally, is a masterclass in this. The streak is a manufactured goal. The little heart-shaped icon that appears when you are about to lose it, is calibrated exquisitely to trigger loss aversion. The reward for continuing is a fresh streak the next day. The whole loop is arrival fallacy on the upside and prospect theory on the downside, wearing a friendly green mascot.

None of this is an argument for giving up on goals. Goals are how the humans live by, and long streaks quietly change us in ways we cannot always see. The French will, eventually, arrive. What the two theories together suggest, though, is that we might do well to spend a little more time enjoying the road, and a little less time worrying about the finish line. Chilling. Sitting. Smelling the roses.

Which reminds me. I do not, actually, have any roses in the house. Let me add that to my list of goals, so that I can then buy them, so that I can then enjoy them, which will in turn make me happy, which will settle down within about seven minutes, at which point I will need to buy more roses or find my next goal.

Somewhere, a small green owl is smiling.

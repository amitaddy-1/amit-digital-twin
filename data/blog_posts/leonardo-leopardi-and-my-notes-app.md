Somewhere on my phone, buried in the Notes app, there is a document that I opened for a specific reason in 2018 and have not been able to close since. It has become a swamp. A gorgeous, embarrassing swamp. In it, in no particular order, sit three lines from a Nassim Taleb book, a bucket list from a trip to Simla, a scribbled thought about mimetic desire, a joke I read about some years ago, two quotes from Ted Lasso, a market research statistic from 2019, and a reminder to read about harness engineering. I have never re-organised it. I keep adding to it. It is, on any honest day, the single most useful document on my phone.

It turns out this swamp has a name, and the name is beautiful. It is called a Zibaldone.

The word is Italian, and I have always felt it shares a kitchen with zabaione, the frothy dessert made by whisking egg yolks, sugar, and wine together into something that tastes better than the sum of its parts. A Zibaldone, applied to ideas, means the same thing. A personal notebook where you throw in everything, no order, no hierarchy, and let the mixture become something bigger than any single ingredient.

The Zibaldone was, in a sense, the Renaissance's most under-appreciated invention. Boccaccio kept one. Leonardo da Vinci filled entire codices with them, and if you have ever seen the pictures of his notebooks up close, you know that a single page might contain a sketch of a flying machine, a shopping list, a doodle of a horse, an anatomical study, and a philosophical question, all fighting for the same square inch. Giacomo Leopardi, the melancholy Italian poet, wrote a Zibaldone that ran to over four thousand pages before he was finished writing. These were not diaries. They were creative soup. Places where things collided.

That collision, I have come to believe, is the whole point. The Zibaldone is not useful because of what is in it. It is useful because of what happens between what is in it. A Kahneman idea sits next to your uncle's joke, and one lends the other something neither would have had on its own. A market research statistic sits next to a TV show quote, and suddenly you can see an insight you had missed for weeks. This is why real creativity is so bad at appointments. Ideas need a place to marinate, a place to sit around each other in strange company. The Zibaldone is that place.

In my own book, Nonlinear, I argued that generative AI could usher in a new Digital Renaissance, and my case rested, in part, on this old, humble notebook. The reason the first Renaissance happened was not that Europeans suddenly became clever. It was that ideas from Greece, Rome, the Arab world, and the emerging vernaculars were suddenly free to bump into each other. The printing press was, in effect, a Zibaldone the size of a continent. When ideas can collide cheaply, incubation happens by default, and the result is a lot of ordinary people quietly becoming a little more inventive than they had planned to be.

Generative AI, at its best, does something similar. It sits, patient and un-embarrassed, on every scrap and quote and half-thought that humanity has ever written down. When I ask it a question, it does what a Zibaldone does when you flip back through it: it finds the surprising neighbour to the idea I walked in with. Sometimes the neighbour is nonsense. Sometimes it is exactly the thing I needed. Either way, the friction is low and the collisions are frequent, which is precisely the recipe.

The next decade, I suspect, will belong to people who keep a Zibaldone of their own alongside the one in their pocket. The Notes app is fine. So is a paper notebook. So is a Google Doc nobody but you will ever open. The form does not matter. The habit does.

So tonight I will add three more messy items to my Notes app. A stray quote from a podcast. A statistic I do not yet understand. A Gen Z term I heard recently, which I am still not sure I have got right. Leonardo would have nodded.

Have you got your own Zibaldone?

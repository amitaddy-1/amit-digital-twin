When I was fifteen, I was absolutely certain that by the time I would be fifty, I would have three things- a flying car, a robot that folded my laundry, and a holiday on the moon. I had this on the authority of Arthur C. Clarke, Isaac Asimov, and the makers of Star Trek: The Next Generation, which at the time felt like reasonable sources.

I am now past fifty and we have none of those things. What I do have is an AI in my pocket that can draft a haiku, explain quantum tunnelling to a ten year old, and help me generate a picture for this blog. The future arrived. It just arrived from the wrong side.

Every science-fiction book of my youth promised the same kind of future: the body side of it. Humanoid robots. Flying cars. Replicators. Protein Synthesizers. Rosie the Maid. Lieutenant Data on the Enterprise delicately arranging cutlery with fingers that could crush titanium. What we actually got, forty years later, is the brain side. Language models that can pass the bar exam. Diffusion models that paint like Van Gogh in three seconds. A chatbot that will now cheerfully help you file your taxes. On the physical side? At best, a robot that occasionally topples over while trying to open a door.

The gap between the sci-fi future and the actual future has an explanation, and it has a name. It was written down in the 1980s by an Austrian-born roboticist named Hans Moravec, and it is now called Moravec's Paradox.

The paradox, put simply, is: what is hard for humans is easy for AI, and what is easy for humans is impossibly hard for AI.

Chess grandmasters have been losing to machines since 1997. Mathematical Olympiads are being cleared by silicon this year. A modern LLM can hold a coherent debate on Kant and then translate it into Marathi. Meanwhile, no robot on earth can reliably fold your bedsheet. No robot can walk into your kitchen and make tea without knocking something over. No robot can babysit a two-year-old for twenty minutes.

Moravec's explanation is insightful. Skills like walking, seeing, grasping, and knowing not to squash an egg are ancient. Evolution has spent nearly a billion years quietly writing them into human neural architecture. They feel effortless to us because a billion years of R&D was already done. Abstract reasoning is new, may be a few thousand years old, riding piggyback on top of that ancient system. We think chess is hard because we struggle at it. We do not think walking is hard because we do not notice how insanely difficult it is under the hood.

Chess sits on a small, well-lit hill. Walking is a rope-climb up a steep mountain that evolution installed hidden ladders on, and we did not even know they were there.

Two implications are worth pausing on, because both flip decades of confident prediction.

The first is that blue-collar work is safer than white-collar work, for now. For a century, the industrial anxiety was that machines would come for factory hands, plumbers, mechanics, and cooks, and that the college-educated brainworker would sit safely on the beach. Moravec's paradox has quietly reversed the poster. The copywriter, the paralegal, the analyst, and the entry-level coder are under real pressure. The plumber, the electrician, the physiotherapist, and my barber are all cheerfully unbothered. A decade from now, the rich will keep plumbers on retainer and the rest of us will keep chatbots.

The second is that self-driving cars are late for a reason nobody in 2016 was ready to say out loud. Driving is not really a thinking problem. It is a perception-and-physical-judgement problem. Handling a plastic bag blowing across the road. A chicken crossing at dusk. A child darting from between two parked cars. These are Moravec problems. Deep, ancient, wired-in problems. That is why the AI that can now write an impressive business plan cannot yet be fully trusted with your daily commute.

So the future is here, and it looks nothing like the poster from 1985. My flying car remains theoretical. My robot laundry-folder is a distant rumour. My holiday on the moon has been rescheduled to later. Instead, I have a chatbot that writes haiku and a plumber who will not answer his phone. Hans Moravec rules.

Something to think about…

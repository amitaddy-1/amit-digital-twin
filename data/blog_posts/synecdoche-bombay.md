I started a new job a few months ago. Given that we are a remote working company, I will say that the coffee is decent (it is my home coffee machine) and the Wi-Fi is faster than I deserve (it is my home Wi-Fi). On Tuesday last week, I was sitting across from a client (at their office)- a warm and rapidly expanding kind of a person from a warm and rapidly expanding kind of an organisation, and he was outlining his plans for the year. Somewhere between his second cup of chai and his third mention of "scaling aggressively", he leaned forward and said the sentence that has stayed with me since.

"We need to put more boots on the ground."

I nodded gravely, which is what one does when clients speak in metaphors. But somewhere at the back of my head, a small librarian sat up and reached for a very old book. Because the sentence I had just heard was not a metaphor. It was a synecdoche.

Let me pause here for a moment, because most people I have met are unsure how to say the word. It is pronounced sih-NEK-duh-kee. Four syllables, gentle emphasis on the second, and the final vowel is a soft "ee" not a "k". I confess I got this wrong for the first two decades of my adult life. I have friends who still say "sin-ek-doach" with great confidence, and I have never had the heart to correct them.

Synecdoche is a very old figure of speech, and one of the most quietly human things we do with language. It is when we let a part stand in for a whole, or a whole stand in for a part. Boots on the ground are, of course, soldiers. But somewhere down the line, someone decided that the boots were the interesting bit. The image landed. The full soldier, backpack and all, was replaced by his footwear. It is a small, poetic act of amputation, and we do it constantly.

Consider how much of our daily speech runs on it. "All hands on deck" (hands for sailors). "Nice wheels" (wheels for a whole car, tyres included, engine included). "We had fifty mouths to feed" (mouths for dependants, and one hopes some ears and eyes came along too). "The White House announced" (a building standing in for a very busy man inside it). "Delhi is not happy" (a city for an entire union government). "Bollywood is celebrating" (an entire industry compressed into two syllables and a suggestion of sequins).

The corporate world runs almost entirely on this stuff. Boots on the ground. Suits in the boardroom. Eyeballs on the campaign. Headcount. Seats to fill. If you listen to a management meeting with the volume turned up, you will notice that half the sentences are body parts and the other half are military equipment. This is a language that describes human beings as an unsettling collage of boots, hands, heads, and eyeballs. Everyone in the room has agreed to pretend this is normal.

Even our tenderest moments come dipped in synecdoche. When your friend says chal, roti khaate hain, he is not really offering you a single flatbread. He is offering the whole meal, with the dal, the sabzi, and possibly the pickle, alongside one honest carbohydrate. When someone gives you a hand, you get, in principle, an entire person's help.

Charlie Kaufman, incidentally, made a whole beautiful film called Synecdoche, New York, about a theatre director who tries to stage his life inside a warehouse and cannot stop building. The word in the title puns on Schenectady, the upstate New York town where the film begins. It is a lovely, exhausting film about a man who mistakes the part for the whole and then keeps building more parts. Which, if you think about it, is a fair description of most of us, most of the time.

So the next time a client leans forward and asks for more boots on the ground, I will nod gravely as before. But quietly, in my head, the small librarian will sit up and smile. Because what the client actually wants is not boots. He wants people. Preferably with a few brains and a couple of spines attached. Preferably not too many suits in the boardroom telling them what to do. And preferably with all their eyeballs firmly on the marketing campaign.

The rest of the meeting, I am pleased to report, went very well. We shook hands.
